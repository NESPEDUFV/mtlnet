# BRIEF A — Expert Reviewer Persona: Multi-Task Learning (MTL)

## Review lenses

1. **Scalarization-first skepticism (the dominant 2022-2026 lens).** The field's null hypothesis is now: *a tuned fixed-weight scalarization (even unitary sum) matches any specialized MTL optimizer.* Kurin et al. showed unitary scalarization + standard regularization (early stopping, weight decay, dropout) matches or beats complex multi-task optimizers in supervised and RL settings ([arXiv:2201.04122](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.04122)); Xin et al. (Google, NeurIPS 2022) showed MTO methods "do not yield any performance improvements beyond what is achievable via traditional optimization" — i.e., a tuned weighted average of losses — and framed comparisons via performance profiles across weightings ([arXiv:2209.11379](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.11379)); Royer et al. replicated this at scale ([arXiv:2310.08910](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.08910)); Hu et al. gave the theoretical account — scalarization traces the Pareto front under mild conditions ([arXiv:2308.13985](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.13985)). A reviewer treats any claim "balancer X beat fixed weights" as unproven until the fixed-weight grid was swept with the same tuning budget.
2. **Mechanism claims about gradient conflict.** Elich et al. ("Examining Common Paradigms in MTL", [arXiv:2311.04698](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04698)) found **no evidence that angular gradient conflict is a uniquely-MTL problem**; gradient *magnitude* differences are the distinguishing factor, and Adam already provides partial loss-scale invariance. Papers that attribute gains to "resolving conflicting gradients" without measuring conflict, or that measure cosine conflict but never correlate it with per-task outcomes, get attacked here.
3. **Negative-transfer claims.** Negative transfer has a formal definition — performance drop on a task relative to training it alone under otherwise equal conditions ([Zhang et al., A Survey on Negative Transfer, arXiv:2009.00909](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.00909); [Wang et al., Characterizing and Avoiding Negative Transfer, arXiv:1811.09751](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.09751)). Reviewers check: is the STL comparator equally tuned (LR, schedule, epochs, regularization)? Is the "gap" stable across seeds? Is it architectural (shared-trunk bottleneck) rather than task interference — and did the authors run the ablation that distinguishes the two?
4. **Capacity matching.** An MTL model vs. N separate STL models is a parameter-count-confounded comparison in both directions. Precedent: Cross-stitch Networks explicitly compared against an *ensemble* baseline with matched total parameters ([arXiv:1604.03539](https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.03539)); "Small Towers Make Big Differences" shows head/trunk capacity allocation itself moves results ([arXiv:2008.05808](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.05808)). Reviewers ask whether "transfer" gains survive giving the STL baseline the same effective capacity/training compute, and whether MTL gains are just implicit regularization on small data.
5. **When-does-MTL-help conditioning.** Task grouping literature (Standley et al., "Which Tasks Should Be Learned Together?", [arXiv:1905.07553](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07553); Fifty et al., TAG, [arXiv:2109.04617](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.04617)) established that transfer-learning affinity does **not** predict MTL affinity and that pairings must be measured, not assumed. A dissertation claiming two tasks "help each other" must show the affinity empirically (pairwise vs. single) and ideally per-direction (A helps B ≠ B helps A).
6. **Checkpoint-selection honesty.** The sharpest protocol attack: reporting each task at its own best epoch describes *two* virtual models, not one. If the thesis claim is "one model, one forward pass, N tasks," reviewers demand a single-checkpoint (joint-selector) number, with the selector defined a priori and applied identically to baselines. Per-task-best numbers are diagnostics only. Xin et al. explicitly discuss the difficulty of reliably evaluating MTO methods (checkpoint and tuning ambiguity) ([NeurIPS 2022 version](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/hash/580c4ec4738ff61d5862a122cdf139b6-Abstract-Conference.html)).
7. **Compute and complexity honesty.** Gradient-surgery methods (PCGrad, CAGrad, Nash-MTL) need per-task gradients → memory/runtime overhead that motivated FAMO ([arXiv:2306.03792](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03792)). Nash-MTL additionally runs an inner convex solver that can fail silently. Reviewers ask for wall-clock/memory columns and solver-failure handling.
8. **Aggregate-metric conventions.** Multi-task results should report per-task deltas vs. STL (Δm% convention from the dense-prediction survey, [Vandenhende et al., arXiv:2004.13379](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13379)), not just a blended scalar that can hide one task being sacrificed.

## Top attack questions

1. Did you sweep fixed task weights (including the unitary sum) with the same HP budget you gave your MTL method — and does your method still win on the performance profile, not just at one weight point?
2. Your STL baselines: tuned independently (own LR/schedule/early-stopping) or run with MTL-tuned hyperparameters? Show the tuning budget per arm.
3. Are your MTL-vs-STL comparisons capacity-matched? What happens if the STL model gets the shared trunk's width, or the MTL trunk is halved?
4. You claim negative transfer / positive transfer — across how many seeds, and is the gap larger than seed variance? Report mean ± std and a test.
5. Is your headline number from a single joint checkpoint, or per-task best epochs? If the latter, you are reporting two models — where is the deployable single-checkpoint result, and how was the selector chosen?
6. What mechanism evidence do you have that gradient conflict (angle) — rather than gradient/loss scale — drives your gap? Did you measure conflict and does it correlate with outcomes ([arXiv:2311.04698](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04698))?
7. Why these two tasks together? Show pairwise task-affinity evidence, both directions, not intuition ([arXiv:1905.07553](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07553)).
8. Did your adaptive balancer's weights actually deviate from ~constant over training? If they converge to a fixed ratio, a static weight replicates it cheaper.
9. What is the wall-clock/memory overhead of your balancer vs. scalarization, and did Nash-MTL's solver ever fail/return degenerate weights during training?
10. Is your "MTL gain" reproducible when the loss weights are re-tuned after any architecture change (weights and architecture interact)?
11. On imbalanced tasks: are both criteria's weighting schemes (class weights, focal) identical across MTL and STL arms, or is a loss-shaping confound driving the "transfer" story?
12. Do you report per-task deltas vs. STL (Δm%-style) or only an average that can hide a sacrificed task?
13. If MTL helps only on small data, have you shown it's transfer and not regularization (e.g., does the gain vanish with more data or with dropout added to STL)?
14. Development-seed contamination: were recipe decisions made on the same seed/split you report? Where are the held-out seeds?
15. Which of the known-negative results do you position against — and if your balancer wins where Kurin/Xin say balancers don't, what is different about your regime (task count, imbalance, heteroscedastic scales)?

Caruana, *Multitask Learning*, MLJ 1997 ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1007379606734)); Ruder overview ([arXiv:1706.05098](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.05098)); uncertainty weighting (Kendall et al., [arXiv:1705.07115](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.07115)); GradNorm ([arXiv:1711.02257](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.02257)); MGDA/Pareto MTL (Sener & Koltun, [arXiv:1810.04650](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04650)); MMoE (Ma et al., KDD 2018); MTAN/DWA ([arXiv:1803.10704](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.10704)); PCGrad ([arXiv:2001.06782](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.06782)); CAGrad ([arXiv:2110.14048](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.14048)); Nash-MTL ([arXiv:2202.01017](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.01017)); FAMO ([arXiv:2306.03792](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03792)); the skeptic block — Kurin ([2201.04122](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.04122)), Xin ([2209.11379](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.11379)), Hu ([2308.13985](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.13985)), Royer ([2310.08910](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.08910)), Elich ([2311.04698](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04698)); task grouping — Standley ([1905.07553](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07553)), Fifty/TAG ([2109.04617](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.04617)); negative transfer survey ([2009.00909](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.00909)); surveys — Zhang & Yang ([1707.08114](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.08114)), Crawshaw ([2009.09796](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.09796)), Vandenhende TPAMI ([2004.13379](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13379)), and the 2024 foundation-era survey ([arXiv:2404.18961](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.18961)).

## Credibility signals an MTL expert trusts

Fixed-weight grid reported alongside the proposed method; equal HP budget stated explicitly per arm; capacity-matched and ensemble baselines; multi-seed with dispersion and significance; single-checkpoint deployable numbers separated from per-task diagnostic bests; Δm%-style per-task tables; compute-cost table; a mechanism section that measures (not asserts) conflict/magnitude; explicit engagement with the Kurin/Xin negative results rather than citing only pro-balancer papers; released code with the exact loss-weight configs.

# BRIEF B — Expert Reviewer Persona: POI / Next-Location Prediction on LBSN data

## Review lenses

1. **Split-protocol legitimacy (the #1 lens).** The field's central documented flaw: non-standardized splits (random vs. temporal, per-user vs. global) cause data leakage and unreliable evaluation ([POI Recommendation: Pitfalls and Viable Solutions, arXiv:2507.13725](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.13725v1)). The strictest realistic setting is a **global temporal split**; Sánchez & Bellogín found only 2 of 17 algorithms (2009-2020) evaluated that way ([ACM CSUR 2022, doi:10.1145/3510409](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3510409); [arXiv:2106.10069](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.10069)). Leave-one-out permits train/test period overlap → temporal leakage ([Time to Split, arXiv:2507.16289](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.16289); [Ji et al., Critical Study on Data Leakage in RecSys Offline Evaluation, arXiv:2010.11060](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.11060)). A reviewer asks precisely: split axis (time/user/random), granularity (per-user or global), and whether *any* future information can reach training.
2. **Window/sequence-construction leakage.** With sliding-window sequence generation, **overlapping windows split randomly across folds put near-duplicate subsequences in train and test**. Reviewers demand: stride disclosed, overlap policy disclosed, and whether stratification is at check-in, window, or user level. Under-documented preprocessing choices (filtering, repeats, cold-start handling) are known to reorder model rankings entirely ([SplitLight, arXiv:2602.19339](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.19339); pitfalls paper Pitfall 19-20).
3. **Transductive-artifact leakage.** Any auxiliary structure — trajectory flow maps (GETNext, [arXiv:2303.04741](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.04741)), transition matrices, hypergraphs, KGs, or *pretrained embeddings* (CTLE, DGI/HGI) — must be built from **training data of the current fold only**. Embeddings trained on the full check-in corpus (including test check-ins/edges) are transductive leakage even if labels never leak; per-fold regeneration is the expected discipline. This is rarely policed in original papers, which is exactly why expert reviewers hunt it in dissertations.
4. **Baseline floors and popularity bias.** Popular/repeat venues are easy to predict; popularity bias inflates accuracy while adding no personalization value (pitfalls paper, Pitfall 13). Human-mobility predictability theory sets known ceilings/floors (Song et al., *Limits of Predictability in Human Mobility*, Science 2010). Reviewers require trivial anchors: persistence (last location), most-frequent-location per user, global popularity top-K, and 1st/2nd-order Markov — the mie-lab benchmark is the reference implementation of this discipline ([Hong et al., TR-C 2023, arXiv:2212.01953](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.01953); [github.com/mie-lab/location-prediction](https://github.com/mie-lab/location-prediction)). They also ask for the **repeat vs. explore breakdown**: how much of Acc@1 is revisitation?
5. **Baseline re-implementation fairness.** The Dacrema et al. finding — most "neural advances" lose to tuned simple baselines ([arXiv:1907.06902](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.06902)) — plus Rendle's "On the Difficulty of Evaluating Baselines" is the default prior. Reviewers ask: were DeepMove/STAN/GETNext re-tuned on *your* preprocessing, or were published numbers copied across incompatible protocols? Known re-implementation traps (e.g., a baseline builder silently using a different stride or min-sequence-length than the proposed model) are exactly what they probe.
6. **Dataset staleness and representativeness.** Gowalla (2009-10) and Foursquare NYC/TKY (2012-13) are over a decade old; POIs have closed/moved; no public post-COVID mobility dataset exists; check-ins are extremely sparse (0.003-0.005% density) and venue-biased (pitfalls paper, Pitfalls 1-5; [Massive-STEPS, arXiv:2505.11239](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.11239) — built explicitly because the field relies on 2012-13 data with poor geographic diversity). Multi-city/multi-region evidence is expected; single-city NYC results are treated as anecdote.
7. **Problem-formulation comparability.** "Next location" is not one task: next-POI vs. next grid/region cell vs. next category change class cardinality by orders of magnitude and make cross-paper numbers incommensurable (survey framing: [Luca et al., ACM CSUR 2021, arXiv:2012.02825](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.02825)). Reviewers demand the label-space size per dataset, how regions/grids were derived, and warn against comparing Acc@10 across different cardinalities. Query-time information usage (does the model see the timestamp of the *target* check-in?) is a known inconsistency across baselines ([VLDB J. benchmarking study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00778-019-00553-0)).
8. **Metric conventions.** Expected: Acc@{1,5,10} (Hit@K), MRR (and/or NDCG) for next-POI; **macro-F1** (not accuracy/weighted-F1 alone) for category prediction because of heavy class imbalance; averaging axis disclosed (per-sample vs. per-user). Beyond-accuracy metrics (novelty/diversity) are a plus (pitfalls paper, Pitfall 16).
9. **Reproducibility.** Code-release rates in POI rec were 13/310 papers, 2011-2020 (pitfalls paper, Pitfall 20). Post-2024 expectation: code + configs + seeds + exact preprocessing, ideally in a benchmark harness (Massive-STEPS, mie-lab, LibCity-style).

## Top attack questions

1. What exactly is your split — temporal, user, or random; per-user or global — and can any check-in later than a test target's time appear in *any* training structure (sequences, graphs, embeddings, transition matrices)?
2. Your sequence windows: stride, overlap, min-length, padding? If windows overlap, how do you prevent near-duplicates straddling the train/test boundary?
3. Were your graph/embedding substrates (flow maps, GCN/DGI/HGI embeddings, CTLE pretraining, transition priors) rebuilt from training data per fold/seed — or trained once on the full corpus (transductive leakage)?
4. Where are the trivial baselines — persistence, per-user most-frequent, popularity top-K, Markov-1/2 — and by how much does your model beat them at Acc@1?
5. What fraction of test targets are revisits of a user's history, and what is your accuracy split on repeat vs. novel POIs?
6. Did you re-tune DeepMove/STAN/GETNext/etc. under *your* preprocessing with a stated budget, or import published numbers from incompatible protocols? Any baseline silently run with different stride/filtering than your model?
7. Your datasets are 2010-2013 Gowalla/Foursquare — why should conclusions transfer to current mobility, and did you test on any recent or geographically diverse corpus ([Massive-STEPS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.11239))?
8. What is the label-space cardinality per dataset/formulation (POIs? regions? categories?), and are your cross-model comparisons at identical cardinality? How were region/grid cells constructed and is that choice ablated?
9. What user/POI filtering (k-core, min check-ins) did you apply, identically across all models — and how sensitive is the ranking to it?
10. Does any model (yours or a baseline) receive the target check-in's timestamp or other query-time information the others don't?
11. Are metrics averaged per-sample or per-user, and does the ranking survive switching the axis? Why macro-F1 (or why not) for the category task?
12. Cold-start: how are unseen users/POIs in the test period handled — dropped (say so, quantify) or predicted?
13. Multi-seed evidence: how many seeds/folds, what dispersion, and were model decisions made on a seed you also report?
14. If you use category prediction as an auxiliary/proxy task, what evidence connects proxy gains to the actual next-POI utility?
15. Can I reproduce your table from the released code alone — configs, seeds, preprocessing scripts, and the exact splits?
