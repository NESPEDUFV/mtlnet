## OUTSTANDING_CRITERIA

**1. Lovitts, "Making the Implicit Explicit" / "How to Grade a Dissertation" (Academe, Nov-Dec 2005) — read in full from the primary PDF (https://www.ittc.ku.edu/~frost/How_to_grade_disseration_Academe_Article.pdf).** Study: 276 faculty, 74 departments, 10 disciplines (incl. electrical & computer engineering), 9 research universities; participants characterized dissertations and components (problem statement, literature review, theory, methods, analysis, discussion/conclusion) at four levels: outstanding / very good / acceptable / unacceptable. Book version: Lovitts 2007, Stylus (https://www.routledge.com/Making-the-Implicit-Explicit-Creating-Performance-Expectations-for-the-Dissertation/Lovitts/p/book/9781579221812).

Table 1, **Outstanding** — verbatim descriptors:
- "Is original and significant, ambitious, brilliant, clear, clever, coherent, compelling, concise, creative, elegant, engaging, exciting, interesting, insightful, persuasive, sophisticated, surprising, and thoughtful"
- "Is very well written and organized"; "Is synthetic and interdisciplinary"; "Connects components in a seamless way"
- "Exhibits mature, independent thinking"; "Has a point of view and a strong, confident, independent, and authoritative voice"
- "Asks new questions or addresses an important question or problem"; "Clearly states the problem and why it is important"
- "Displays a deep understanding of a massive amount of complicated literature"; "Exhibits command and authority over the material"
- "Argument is focused, logical, rigorous, and sustained"; "Is theoretically sophisticated and shows a deep understanding of theory"
- "Has a brilliant research design"; "Uses or develops new tools, methods, approaches, or types of analyses"
- "Is thoroughly researched"; "Has rich data from multiple sources"; "Analysis is comprehensive, complete, sophisticated, and convincing"; "Results are significant"
- "Conclusion ties the whole thing together"
- "Is publishable in top-tier journals"; "Is of interest to a larger community and changes the way people think"; "Pushes the discipline's boundaries and opens new areas for research"

Narrative gloss (same article): outstanding dissertations "defied explication — you know it when you see it"; they come along "once or twice a decade"; characterized by "originality, high-quality writing, and compelling consequences"; components "integrated seamlessly"; faculty reaction is "Wow! Why didn't I think of that?"; they are "page turners." Producers are "very creative and intellectually adventurous… They leap into new territory and transfer ideas from place to place."

Table 1, **Very Good** (the contrast class — this is where "merely good" lives): "Is solid… distinguished by being 'less' — less original, less significant, less ambitious, less exciting, and less interesting than the outstanding category"; "Has a good question or problem that tends to be small and traditional"; "Is the next step in a research program (good normal science)"; "Uses appropriate (standard) theory, methods, and techniques"; "Obtains solid, expected results or answers"; "Misses opportunities to completely explore interesting issues and connections"; "Makes a modest contribution to the field but does not open it up"; "The work expands rather than alters the thinking of a field." Crucially: "One or more components of the dissertation may not be as strong as the others." **Acceptable** = "workmanlike," "not very — not very original, significant, exciting"; "highly derivative or an extension of the adviser's work"; "reviews the literature adequately… but is not critical of it or does not discuss what is important"; "unsophisticated analysis — does not explore all possibilities and misses connections"; "predictable results"; "adds little to the field." Lovitts also notes the intended target is *very good*, "because very good is the level most faculty members expect of most graduate students. Done is better than perfect."

Table 2 (component dimensions the rubric checks): Introduction — problem statement, clear research question, motivation, context, summary of findings, importance, roadmap. Literature review — "comprehensive and up to date," "command of the literature," "contextualizes the problem," "selective, synthetic, analytical, and thematic" discussion. Methods — appropriate, described in detail, aligned with question and theory, plus demonstrated understanding of "the methods' advantages and disadvantages." Results/Analysis — appropriate, aligned with question/hypotheses, iterative, sufficient data, well presented, "intelligently interpreted," and the author "cogently expresses the insights gained" AND "the study's limitations." Discussion/Conclusion — summarizes, gives perspective, "refers back to the introduction," "ties everything together," "discusses the study's strengths and weaknesses," implications for the discipline, future directions.

**2. Mullins & Kiley 2002, "'It's a PhD, not a Nobel Prize'" (Studies in Higher Education 27(4)) — read in full (https://documents.uow.edu.au/content/groups/public/@web/@raid/documents/doc/uow016364.pdf).** 30 experienced examiners. Key findings for an excellence assessor:
- **Outstanding = artistic metaphor**: "an artistic endeavour where the student is designing the work and there is elegance of design, of the synthesis, and executions"; "creativity"; "design — where it all fits together"; "elegant"; "a well-sculpted piece of work"; plus "élan, passion, excitement, sparkle." One examiner: "The outstanding PhDs have beautifully conceived ideas that open up a new area or really answer an important question, and are critical of previous work in the area or make a critical assessment of their own work." Another: "You form an impression that it is OK but not dazzling… when the student applies standard theories in a rather pedestrian way… In other cases you can see that the material is taken and used originally at every level — methodology, literature review, etc. Right from the beginning it makes you see an area that you thought you knew in a way that you hadn't thought about before."
- **First impressions**: judgment of quality "usually formed by the end of the second or third chapter — often by the end of the literature review." "A good indicator is the way the candidate reviews the literature and their overall grasp of what's going on"; "It is unusual that if someone does a poor job of the literature review that they will suddenly improve, or vice versa."
- **Final substantive judgment determined by**: "the student's confidence and independence; a creative view of the topic; the structure of the argument; the coherence of theoretical and methodological perspectives; and evidence of critical self-assessment by the student." Positive indicators: "sparkle, élan and sense of confidence with the material"; "cohesiveness and clarity"; "a student who makes the ideas his/her own"; "professionalism — mature comments, accuracy of the logic"; "style and sophistication."
- **'Substantial' quantified in the sciences** as "the number of journal articles likely to arise from the work… two to four good journal articles" — publishability as the quantity/quality yardstick.
- **Publications influence**: "Half our sample explicitly acknowledged that they were favourably influenced by the fact that a candidate's work had been accepted for publication in a reputable journal"; "It immediately suggests the student deserves the degree"; "If there are two or three good publications you can put your feet up… If there is nothing published you think 'That's interesting'!" (Minority dissent: "The examiner is there to examine that piece of work, not anything else.")
- **Reflection/critical self-assessment** as an excellence marker: "They make a critical assessment of their own work"; "How they recognise and deal with contradictions"; "An appreciation of what the research means."
- Examiners check "the link between the introduction, where students outline their intentions, and the conclusions, where the intentions should have been realised."

**3. Holbrook & Bourke corpus (804/301 real examiner reports, Australia):**
- Holbrook, Bourke, Lovat & Dally 2004, "Investigating PhD thesis examination reports" (IJER 41(2):98-120; https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0883035505000212) — content analysis linking report text categories to recommendations; examiner comment most extensively devoted to analysis and reporting of findings; the paper explores "the assessment discourse of examiners with reference to high- and low-quality theses."
- Holbrook, Bourke, Fairbairn & Lovat 2007, "Examiner comment on the literature review in Ph.D. theses" (Studies in Higher Education 32(3):337-356; https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/03075070701346899): ~1/10 of a report is about the literature; the decisive quality split is **coverage vs. use** — "a pass requires coverage and a working understanding of the literature; but, for a good thesis, a candidate must critically engage with the literature"; examiners use the literature review as a "litmus test for the quality of the thesis as a whole."
- Bourke & Holbrook 2013, "Examining PhD and research masters theses" (AEHE 38(4); https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/02602938.2011.638738): tested whether examiners treat masters vs PhD differently on 12 indicators across contribution (originality, substantive contribution, advancing knowledge), literature review, approach/methodology, analyses/results, presentation; the 12 indicators individually and collectively were "strongly related to the holistic assessment of thesis quality" — i.e., the same excellence dimensions operate at master's level, with contribution weighted somewhat less than at PhD.
- Golding, Sharmini & Lazarovitch 2014, "What examiners do: what thesis students should know" (AEHE 39(5):563-576; https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02602938.2013.859230) — synthesis of the examiner literature: examiners read like reviewers, expect a thesis to pass, judge heavily on coherence and critical engagement, and are disproportionately irritated by presentation errors.
- Dally, Holbrook, Lovat & Budd 2019 (Australian Universities' Review 61(2), read in full via https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1228240.pdf): institutional examination criteria across 19 universities converge on "a systematic and comprehensive literature review; effective and rigorous methodology appropriate to the thesis topic; results presented in an accurate and logical manner; and a lucid discussion and conclusions that are linked to the research questions," plus "the thesis (or parts thereof) had to be suitable for publication and the literary presentation was expected to be clear, discernible, coherent, accurately and cogently written, concise and authoritative." Notably (Monfries & Lovat 2006, cited therein): even reports for **top-rated theses** show "a pervading theme of deficit" — examiners critique everything; an excellence assessor should not read presence-of-criticism as absence-of-excellence.

**4. Publication-based / coletânea theses (Sharmini line):**
- Sharmini, Spronken-Smith, Golding & Harland 2015, "Assessing the doctoral thesis when it includes published work" (AEHE 40(1); https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02602938.2014.888535): survey of 62 examiners of publication-based theses (PBTs). 69% voiced concerns about **the candidate's intellectual input in multi-authored papers**; coherence of the whole was the other main concern; 86% "highly influenced by publications in top-ranked journals and international peer-reviewed journals"; 48% found PBTs easier to assess, 26% wanted more guidance, 29% used their own extended criteria.
- Sharmini & Kumar 2018, "Examiners' commentary on thesis with publications" (Innovations in Education and Teaching International; https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1193409): examiners comment as much as on traditional theses, give more formative feedback than summative, **still treat published chapters as work-in-progress and expect changes to them**; criticisms center on "lacking internal consistency" and "lack of coherence and cohesion in the thesis article portfolio"; strong PBTs have a substantial introduction with literature review, **linking material between publications to contextualise and integrate each submission**, and a concluding synthesis.
- Successor: "'A thesis is a thesis'? Insights into how researchers examine doctoral theses that include publications" (2025; https://www.researchgate.net/publication/390179606) — examiners apply thesis-level (not journal-level) criteria to the compilation as a whole.
- Brazilian-context structural guidance: "Paper-based thesis and dissertations: analysis of fundamental characteristics for achieving a robust structure" (https://www.redalyc.org/journal/3967/396766539008/html/) — general introduction, explicit inter-chapter bridges, and integrated conclusions as the load-bearing elements.
- Policy warning found in Dally et al. 2019: candidates in Australian universities are explicitly told "having published sections of the thesis in a peer-reviewed format is not an adequate defence for not actioning suggested changes" — peer review does not immunize chapters from examiner criticism.

## AWARD_RUBRICS

**ACM Doctoral Dissertation Award** (https://awards.acm.org/doctoral-dissertation/nominations; wording confirmed via https://www.grad.ubc.ca/awards/acm-doctoral-dissertation-award and https://scsdean.cs.cmu.edu/acm/acm-scs-dissertation.html): dissertations are reviewed for "**technical depth and significance of the research contribution, potential impact on theory and practice, and quality of presentation**." Nominations require an endorsement letter plus 3-5 expert supporting letters attesting to impact — i.e., external validation of significance is part of the mechanism, not just the text.

**SBC CTD — Concurso de Teses e Dissertações, CSBC 2026** (https://csbc.sbc.org.br/2026/index.php/ctd/): selects the best Brazilian CS master's dissertations and PhD theses of the year (10 MSc + 6 PhD finalists; 3+3 awarded plus 2 honorable mentions). Evaluation: "qualidade científica e tecnológica dos trabalhos," "contribuições para a Computação," "potencial de impacto" (on society and on the state of the art), with earlier editions phrasing it as "relevância e grau de dificuldade do problema, resultados obtidos, impacto no estado da arte da Computação, e as publicações associadas (qualificação e relevância dos veículos)" (https://csbc.sbc.org.br/2024/ctd/). Submission = 10-page research summary (problem characterization, objectives, contributions, related work, results, methodology) + a **products list** (papers, patents, software, datasets) + full dissertation + diploma. At least 3 specialist reviewers per work; final award also weighs the oral presentation. Note the structural implication: a top Brazilian dissertation must be summarizable in 10 pages with a crisp problem/contribution/impact story, and carries an explicit artifact/publication inventory.

**SBRC CTD 2026** (networks/distributed systems; https://sbrc.sbc.org.br/2026/concurso-de-teses-e-dissertacoes/): same architecture — >=3 reviewers evaluate "o trabalho completo" via extended abstract (10 pp), **derived publications/byproducts document (6 pp)**, and full text; criteria = scientific/technological results already generated AND future potential, impact on the field's state of the art.

**CAPES Prêmio de Tese, Edital 14/2026** (read in full: https://www.gov.br/capes/pt-br/centrais-de-conteudo/editais/23042026_Edital_2806987_SEI_2806876_Edital_14_2026.pdf) — the explicit national scoring table (Section 4.1, each 0-10 in 0.5 steps):
- I. "Originalidade do trabalho" — **peso 2**
- II. "Relevância para o desenvolvimento científico, e/ou tecnológico/econômico, e/ou político/social, e/ou saúde e bem-estar, e/ou ambiental" — **peso 2**
- III. "Robustez e/ou reprodutibilidade da metodologia e análise de dados utilizados na tese" — peso 1
- IV. "Qualidade da redação, estrutura/organização do texto" — peso 1
- V. "Grau de inovação e/ou interdisciplinaridade da tese" — peso 1
- VI. "Qualidade e impacto(s) de produtos oriundos da tese" — peso 1
Tie-breaks: originality first, then relevance. Committees must write a justifying parecer per thesis, and may **decline to award** if nothing reaches "um patamar de alta qualidade." Submissions may attach "exemplares digitais de artigos e/ou livros aceitos para publicação, ou ainda outros produtos relevantes decorrentes da tese" for the committee to consider. Also structurally relevant: internal program committees must pre-select ONE thesis verifying "o elevado patamar de qualidade exigido" — excellence is comparative and scarce by design. (Criteria summary corroborated at https://www.gov.br/capes/pt-br/assuntos/premios/premio-capes-de-tese.)

Synthesis across the three Brazilian instruments: **originality × relevance are double-weighted; reproducibility/robustness is an explicit named criterion; writing quality is a scored criterion; and "produtos oriundos" (publications in strong venues, software, datasets) are a first-class evidence channel.** For a coletânea dissertation, the venue tier of the constituent papers effectively functions as pre-scored evidence on criteria I, II, and VI.

## CS_SPECIFIC_SIGNALS

- **ACM's award triad** ("technical depth and significance… potential impact on theory and practice… quality of presentation") is the canonical CS committee formula — note that presentation is co-equal, and "theory AND practice" impact rewards work that both explains and ships (https://awards.acm.org/doctoral-dissertation/nominations).
- **Artifacts and reproducibility as excellence currency**: ACM's formal Artifact Review and Badging policy defines the ladder committees now recognize — Artifacts Available, Artifacts Evaluated (Functional / Reusable), Results Reproduced/Replicated (https://www.acm.org/publications/policies/artifact-review-and-badging-current). A top ML dissertation today reads as if it could earn "Reusable + Results Reproduced": pinned environments, seeds, exact commands, released code/data. (Caveat documented in the field: >40% of "functional" artifacts rot within months from unpinned dependencies — durability engineering is itself a distinguishing signal; https://arxiv.org/html/2512.00651v1.)
- **Statistical rigor and honest claims-evidence matching**: the NeurIPS/ICML/ICLR paper checklists institutionalize what committees reward — "claims made should match theoretical and experimental results, and reflect how much the results can be expected to generalize"; strong assumptions must be surfaced with robustness-to-violation discussion; limitations discussion is mandatory and answering "No" honestly "is not grounds for rejection" (https://aclrollingreview.org/responsibleNLPresearch/ builds on Dodge et al., "Show Your Work: Improved Reporting of Experimental Results," EMNLP 2019 — report variance across seeds, budget-matched comparisons, hyperparameter search disclosure).
- **Honest negative results**: the examiner literature converges with ML norms here — Lovitts (experimental disciplines): otherwise-good dissertations are still acceptable "when experiment(s) do not work out and students get null or negative results"; what distinguishes outstanding is *what the author does with them* — Mullins & Kiley's examiners prize "how they recognise and deal with contradictions" and "critical assessment of their own work." In ML terms: a falsified hypothesis with a diagnosed mechanism (e.g., an fp16 artifact or a confound dissolved) is excellence evidence; an unexplained dip is not.
- **Publications as pre-validation**: Mullins & Kiley — half of examiners explicitly favorably influenced by acceptance in a reputable journal ("a check as well as a tick"; it "lightens the burden… other reviewers have said that it is OK"); sciences examiners quantify a strong thesis as "two to four good journal articles" of material. For a three-paper coletânea, venue tier + the candidate's demonstrable intellectual ownership (cf. Sharmini's 69% co-authorship concern) are the two levers.
- **The crisp single-sentence contribution**: SBC CTD's 10-page summary format and CAPES' 500-character abstract force it; Lovitts' outstanding descriptor "Clearly states the problem and why it is important" and the examiner question "Is this actually 'research' — is there an argument?" (Mullins & Kiley) are the assessor-side test: can the contribution be stated in one falsifiable sentence, and does every chapter visibly serve it?

## ACTIONABLE_RUBRIC

Ten dimensions; each: GOOD → OUTSTANDING, with text-level evidence an assessor should look for. Calibrated for a master's coletânea (Bourke & Holbrook 2013: same dimensions as PhD, contribution threshold proportionately lower — but award-level master's work is judged by the PhD-shaped instruments above).

1. **Problem framing & significance** (Lovitts; CAPES I+II peso 2). GOOD: clear, cited problem statement; "next step in a research program." OUTSTANDING: "asks a new question or addresses an important problem," and the introduction argues *why now / why hard / why it matters* beyond the lab — the reader can quote the problem in one sentence. Evidence: a single load-bearing thesis statement early in the general introduction; a paragraph tracing consequences if solved; the problem is NOT "an extension of the adviser's work" in framing.
2. **Contribution clarity & unity (coletânea-critical)** (Sharmini 2018; Lovitts "connects components in a seamless way"). GOOD: three sound papers, an introduction and a conclusion. OUTSTANDING: one intellectual arc that the papers *instantiate* — the general introduction states a claim no single paper makes alone, bridge texts between chapters say what each paper established and what question it forced next, and the conclusion answers the introduction's question (Mullins & Kiley: examiners explicitly check intro-conclusion linkage). Evidence: explicit "Chapter N showed X, which raised Y, addressed in Chapter N+1" connective tissue; a contributions table mapping papers → thesis-level claims.
3. **Command & critical use of literature** (Holbrook 2007 coverage-vs-use; Lovitts). GOOD: comprehensive, current, correctly attributed coverage. OUTSTANDING: "selective, synthetic, analytical, thematic" — the review *takes positions*, identifies what is important and what is weak, contextualizes the problem, and "tells a story" (Mullins & Kiley). Evidence: evaluative verbs ("overstates," "conflates," "cannot explain"); a synthesis figure/taxonomy of the field; gaps that map one-to-one onto the thesis's research questions. This is the examiner's litmus test — judged by chapter 2-3.
4. **Methodological rigor & justification** (Lovitts methods dimensions; CAPES III). GOOD: appropriate, detailed, standard methods. OUTSTANDING: design choices defended against alternatives with the "advantages and disadvantages" of each; protocol decisions (splits, metrics, baselines) justified before results; threats to validity anticipated in-design, not post-hoc. Evidence: "we chose A over B because…" sentences; leakage/confound guards described; ablations that isolate each claim.
5. **Statistical & empirical rigor (ML-specific)** (NeurIPS checklist; Dodge et al.). GOOD: standard metrics, one seed, best-run tables. OUTSTANDING: multi-seed with variance, significance or effect-size reasoning, budget-matched baselines, dev/report-seed separation, claims scoped exactly to evidence ("claims match results and reflect generalization limits"). Evidence: ± std in every headline table; explicit hyperparameter search disclosure; sentences that *downgrade* their own generality where data is thin.
6. **Originality & insight** (CAPES peso 2; Lovitts; Mullins & Kiley "material used originally at every level"). GOOD: solid, expected results — "expands rather than alters the thinking of a field." OUTSTANDING: reframes — after reading, the assessor sees "an area you thought you knew in a way you hadn't thought about before"; new tools/analyses developed, ideas transferred across areas. Evidence: a named mechanism/finding that survives beyond the specific benchmark; a section that overturns a plausible prior belief with evidence.
7. **Critical self-assessment & honest negatives** (Mullins & Kiley "reflection"; Lovitts "discusses the study's strengths and weaknesses"). GOOD: a limitations section listing generic caveats. OUTSTANDING: specific, consequential limitations tied to claims ("finding F holds only under S; here is the experiment that would break it"), negative and null results reported with diagnosed mechanisms, contradictions between own chapters confronted rather than hidden. Evidence: limitations that could only have been written by someone who ran the experiments; a "what we got wrong / what changed our mind" thread.
8. **Reproducibility & artifacts** (CAPES III "reprodutibilidade"; ACM badging; SBC products list). GOOD: methods described well enough to reimplement in principle. OUTSTANDING: released code/data/configs with pinned versions, exact commands, seeds; artifact inventory in the dissertation itself; results tied to specific artifact versions. Evidence: a reproducibility statement or appendix; repository links per chapter; dataset/hash documentation.
9. **Writing, structure, voice** (Lovitts "authoritative voice," "page turners"; ACM "quality of presentation"; CAPES IV). GOOD: clear, correct, well organized. OUTSTANDING: confident, economical prose with "a point of view"; signposting that makes the argument's spine visible; zero sloppiness — Mullins & Kiley: typos and reference errors "flip" examiners because "sloppiness in the text indicates sloppy research," and first impressions form by chapter 2-3. Evidence: error-free front matter, bibliography, and cross-references; consistent notation across the three papers (unified after compilation, not left in per-venue dialects); an abstract that states problem-approach-result-implication in <=200 words.
10. **External validation & impact trail** (Mullins & Kiley publications finding; SBC/SBRC/CAPES "produtos"; ACM impact letters). GOOD: papers submitted or accepted somewhere. OUTSTANDING: venue tier commensurate with claims, plus non-paper products (software, datasets, benchmarks) and a visible candidate-ownership statement for each co-authored paper (Sharmini: 69% of examiners worry about intellectual input). Evidence: a per-chapter provenance note ("this chapter corresponds to [paper], where the candidate designed X, implemented Y, wrote Z"); a consolidated products list mirroring the CTD format.

Two cross-cutting assessor tests: (a) **the chapter-2 test** — quality judgment forms by the end of the literature review; if the general introduction + review don't establish authority, nothing later recovers it; (b) **the intro-conclusion loop test** — do the conclusions "take off from where the first chapter ends," realizing exactly the intentions announced?

## ANTI_PATTERNS

Documented reasons otherwise-strong theses miss the top rating:
1. **Stapled compilation** (Sharmini 2018): "lack of coherence and cohesion in the thesis article portfolio," "lacking internal consistency" — papers bound without linking material, contradictory notation/assumptions across chapters, no thesis-level claim above the papers. The most cited PBT failure mode.
2. **Co-authorship opacity** (Sharmini 2015): 69% of examiners doubt candidate's intellectual input in multi-authored chapters when contribution statements are absent or boilerplate.
3. **"Published = untouchable" defense** (Dally et al. 2019, institutional policy): "having published sections… in a peer-reviewed format is not an adequate defence for not actioning suggested changes" — treating acceptance as immunity reads as unreflective; examiners still treat published chapters as work-in-progress (Sharmini 2018).
4. **Catalog literature review** (Holbrook 2007; Lovitts "acceptable"): coverage without use — "knows the literature but is not critical of it or does not discuss what is important"; annotated-bibliography structure (one paragraph per paper) instead of thematic argument. Doubly damaging because the review is the whole-thesis litmus test.
5. **Sloppiness cascade** (Mullins & Kiley): typos, calculation slips, broken references "flip" the examiner from reasonable to unreasonable — "If there are mistakes in calculations then that starts one thinking… you start to read it differently." Presentation errors are read as proxies for research errors.
6. **Adviser's-extension problem** (Lovitts "acceptable"): question "highly derivative or an extension of the adviser's work"; no independent point of view or authoritative voice.
7. **Contribution inflation / claims-evidence mismatch** (Lovitts "unacceptable": "unsupported or exaggerated interpretation"; NeurIPS checklist: claims must match results and generalization limits). Overselling a modest delta as a paradigm shift is the fastest way to lose an expert committee; examiners want theses that "pre-empt inevitable critiques."
8. **Unreflective limitations section** (contrast with Mullins & Kiley's prized "critical assessment of their own work"): generic caveats ("more data would help") instead of claim-scoped, mechanism-aware limitations; hiding or not explaining negative results rather than diagnosing them (Lovitts: null results are survivable; unexplained/misinterpreted results are "unacceptable"-tier).
9. **Broken intro-conclusion contract** (Mullins & Kiley): announcing intentions the conclusion never revisits — examiners "check carefully for the link between the introduction… and the conclusions, where the intentions should have been realised"; also not "doing what one said one would do, or explaining how and why changes were made."
10. **Uneven components** (Lovitts "very good"): "one or more components not as strong as the others" is precisely what demotes outstanding to very good — in a coletânea, typically a weak general introduction/conclusion wrapped around strong papers, or one clearly weaker paper dragging the arc.
11. **Missed connections** (Lovitts "very good"/"acceptable"): "misses opportunities to completely explore interesting issues and connections" — in a multi-study dissertation, failing to run the cross-study synthesis the structure begs for.
12. **Deficit-reading trap for assessors** (Monfries & Lovat 2006 via Dally 2019): even top-rated theses' reports show "a pervading theme of deficit"; an excellence assessor must score against the positive descriptors, not count criticisms.
